Supplemental Materials: Party or Climate? The Polarization of the US Public’s Energy Preferences

Methods and Robustness Checks

Survey Sample

Despite the use of quotas, the sample from Cloud Research was not perfectly representative of the US population. Specifically, the sample was younger, whiter, more educated, and more aligned with the Democratic Party than the US population. To address this, I used the survey and anesrake packages in R to perform iterative raking to compute survey weights that align the sample with the US Census’s 2023 American Community Survey (ACS) on key demographic variables, including age, race, gender, and education. Additionally, I used the Pew Research Center’s National Public Opinion Reference Survey (NPORS) from 2025 to align the sample on political party affiliation.1 The differences between the unweighted and weighted samples are shown in Table 1.

Robustness: Results Without Survey Weights

The analysis presented in the manuscript used the survey weights. To confirm that the main results are not a result of the weights, I re-estimate the preferred-mix models with unweighted OLS using the same specification (party * treatment for each energy source). Table 2 replicates the pairwise-differences table from the main text, and Table 3 replicates the difference-in-differences table. The estimates are substantively unchanged from the weighted analysis, indicating that the cue effects are likely not a product of the weights.

Covariate Balance Across Conditions

Respondents were randomly assigned to the Trump, climate, or control conditions. Table 4 compares the demographic covariates across the three conditions to confirm that the random assignment produced comparable groups. Of the covariates examined, the conditions differed significantly only in terms of age and race; however, political beliefs, the variables of interest, were balanced across conditions.

Robustness: Controlling for Demographic Covariates

Because of the differences in age and race across the conditions, I re-estimated the difference-in-differences models with age, sex, race, education, and income added as controls. Table 5 and Table 6 report largely the same results as the main-text pairwise-differences and difference-in-differences tables. The estimated cue effects on the partisan gap are substantively unchanged from what is shown in the manuscript, indicating that the results are likely not driven by the age and race imbalances across conditions.

Preregistered Hypotheses

In addition to the hypotheses presented in the manuscript, I also preregistered other hypotheses that were not included in the analysis. First, were hypotheses regarding political beliefs on support/opposition to the climate change and Trump cues. Second, were hypotheses about the impacts of the cues on the preferred energy mix for all respondents (i.e., not split by political beliefs).

Cue Hypotheses

Conservative Republicans will be more supportive of Trump’s actions to increase the use of fossil fuels and decrease the use of renewable sources than those with other political beliefs

Liberal Democrats will be more supportive of increasing the use of renewable sources to address climate change than those with other political beliefs

The results presented in Figure 1 in the manuscript show that these hypotheses were confirmed.

Energy Mix All Respondents Hypotheses

Those who receive the Trump cue will support a higher percentage of fossil fuels than those in the climate change or control group

Those who receive the climate cue will support a higher percentage of renewable energy sources (wind, solar, hydropower) than those in the Trump or control group

Table 7 and Table 8 show the mean preferred percentage for each energy source under each treatment condition, pooled across all respondents, weighted (Table 7) and unweighted (Table 8). The p-value in each row tests whether the three condition means differ for that energy source (a design-based F-test for the weighted table, a one-way ANOVA for the unweighted table).

As shown, respondents in the Trump condition preferred more fossil fuels than those in the other conditions, with or without survey weights. In addition, the climate condition showed some increases in renewable sources, but these results were not robust, with p-values <0.10 and differences between the weighted and unweighted samples.
